The mission

Make user simulation a capability the field can trust

Help establish the evidence, standards, and open tools needed to make user simulation diverse, faithful, and trustworthy.

The problem

Simulated users can be unreliable proxies of real users

User simulation has quietly become infrastructure. Simulated users now stand in for real ones across agentic evaluation, conversational search, dialogue systems and recommenders, and they have moved into training pipelines at frontier labs as sources of synthetic interaction data, preference and process-reward signal, and as reinforcement learning environments in their own right.

That makes the simulator a measurement instrument, and measurement instruments need calibration. Recent work finds that measured agent success can move by as much as nine points depending only on which model plays the user, that overly cooperative simulators inflate performance, and that simulators are miscalibrated for speakers of dialects including African American Vernacular English and Indian English.

The opportunity is just as consequential. Well-grounded simulation can make interactive evaluation reproducible, cover rare and long-tail behavior, and extend evaluation to multilingual and sovereign-AI systems with little in-language production traffic. Population-scale grounding is becoming feasible just as agent training and evaluation are becoming more dependent on simulated interaction. The field needs shared evidence and reporting norms now, before weak assumptions harden into standard practice.

This workshop brings together the communities building and stress-testing these simulators around a single object, the simulated user itself, and one question: when can we trust it?

Who it’s for

Across communities, one question

User simulation has advanced in parallel across fields that rarely meet, under different names and with no shared standards. We are convening them around the same object.

  • Agentic LLM evaluation
  • Information retrieval and conversational search
  • Dialogue and task-oriented systems
  • HCI and social simulation
  • Reinforcement learning and recommender systems
  • Synthetic data and model training
  • Multilingual and lower-resource NLP
  • Fairness, responsible AI and evaluation methodology

If you build simulators, validate or audit them, or use them to train and evaluate models, this workshop is for you. Work that stress-tests simulation is as welcome as work that advances it. Negative results, replications, and evidence that a simulator fails to transfer are first-class contributions.

  • The first dedicated NeurIPS venue for user simulation. Unlike broader agent and environment workshops, this one centers the simulated user itself: persona fidelity, Sim2Real gaps, fairness, and representativeness.
  • Broader than IR-specific efforts. It builds on Sim4IA and TREC, but brings the topic to the ML community across agentic LLMs, IR, dialogue, HCI and social simulation, RL, and recommenders.
  • Evaluation and training together. It connects simulation as an evaluation substrate with simulation as training data, preference and process-reward signal, and RL environment, while pairing simulator builders with validity-and-fairness skeptics.

Mission and pillars

Accelerating user simulation as an open, rigorous, shared capability

We organize the agenda around three pillars, covering the two strategically important uses of simulation: evaluation of interactive and agentic systems, and training via SFT data, preference and process-reward signals, and simulators as RL environments.

Diversity

The range of users, languages, cultures, goals, tasks, and scenarios represented, so simulation spans the real population rather than a narrow slice.

Fidelity

Whether simulated users match real individual and population-level behavior, including style, strategy, natural variation, and the tendency of real users to be incomplete, inconsistent, impatient, or strategic rather than idealized and cooperative.

Validity

Whether conclusions drawn from simulation transfer to reality: do simulated evaluations predict real outcomes, and is the training signal trustworthy?

The centerpiece

“Can we trust
simulated users?”

A moderated debate sits at the heart of the program, pairing the people building simulators with the people stress-testing them. We are deliberately seating builders opposite skeptics so the day produces shared standards rather than shared enthusiasm.

The goal is not a yes-or-no verdict. It is a practical account of which claims simulation can support, what validation those claims require, and which failure modes every evaluation should disclose.

What you get out of it

Shared infrastructure, not just a program

We build on open community efforts rather than starting from scratch. Participants should leave with reusable artifacts and agreement on what a trustworthy simulator ought to report.

  • A pilot baseline user simulator

    An open, census-conditioned baseline to build on, extend or argue with, released with its persona and configuration scripts.

  • A simulator reporting standard

    A validation checklist and failure-mode taxonomy: which simulator was used, how it was validated, on which populations, and what class of claim the results can support.

  • A living benchmark catalog

    A maintained index of open benchmarks, datasets and evaluation environments for user simulation across communities.

  • A community calibration exercise

    A non-competitive first-year exercise over selected scenarios we will release from the TREC 2026 User Simulation Track and the τ-bench family, with a fairness and representativeness lens. Bring any simulator: the aim is shared calibration, not a winner.

  • A community report

    Written breakout notes consolidated into a short public report on what the field agrees a trustworthy simulator should demonstrate.

The exercise is open to any simulator, and the catalog covers work from across the field, including SOTOPIA, UserBench and ConvApparel, not only the benchmarks the organizers happen to have built. Participation details will be announced with the scenarios. A future edition may expand this foundation into a competitive leaderboard; Year 1 is about agreeing on the evidence a trustworthy simulator should report.

Topics

Diversity

  • Population grounding, representativeness and census-conditioned personas
  • Multilingual, cross-cultural and lower-resource user simulation
  • Scenario and goal generation; coverage of rare and long-tail interactions
  • Demographic and dialectal calibration of simulators

Fidelity

  • Behavioral realism and cognitive plausibility
  • Individual-level versus distributional fidelity, and the metrics each requires
  • Behavioral variance, entropy and mode collapse in simulated populations
  • Persona adherence, steerability and consistency over long interactions
  • Modeling users who are incomplete, inconsistent, impatient or strategic

Validity

  • Sim2Real validation against held-out human studies
  • Relative, system-ranking validity versus absolute validity
  • Identifiability: separating agent capability from simulator behavior
  • Contamination, reward hacking and Goodhart effects when training against a simulator
  • Reporting norms and disclosure standards for simulation-based evaluation

Across all three

  • Simulation for training: synthetic interaction data, preference and process rewards, simulators as RL environments
  • Open benchmarks, environments and tooling for building and scaling simulators
  • Agentic and tool-use user simulation; interactive and trajectory-level evaluation
  • Shared terminology and problem taxonomies that transfer across communities

Responsible use

Simulation can reduce harm—or hide it

User simulation can support privacy-preserving evaluation, scalable safety testing, and disaggregated fairness analysis without repeatedly exposing real people to risk. But simulators can also inherit bias, reward systems for exploiting their quirks, or create an “easy mode” that masks failures real users would encounter.

That tension is central to the workshop. We are interested not only in stronger simulators, but in validation and reporting practices that make the limits of simulation visible.

Before you ask

Questions

Do I need to be registered for NeurIPS Paris to submit?

No. Submissions are open to everyone, wherever you are and whether or not you attend. NeurIPS 2026 runs across Sydney, Paris and Atlanta and attendees choose a site; this workshop is held in Paris. Location matters only for presenting, not for submitting or being accepted.

What if no author can attend in Paris?

Your paper is still reviewed and, if accepted, listed on this site and on OpenReview. We expect at least one author to present in person, so a paper with no author in Paris will not be allocated a poster slot, but acceptance itself does not depend on attendance.

Is the workshop archival?

No. The workshop is non-archival and authors retain all rights. You are free to submit the work to an archival venue afterwards.

Can I submit work that is currently under review elsewhere?

Yes. Because the workshop is non-archival, concurrent submission is permitted. Please check that the other venue permits it as well.

Is previously published work eligible? What about main-track NeurIPS 2026 papers?

Neither is eligible. We ask authors to attest that the work is not previously published and is not appearing in the NeurIPS 2026 main track.

I already have a preprint on arXiv. Does that disqualify me?

No. Preprints are explicitly allowed. Please anonymize your submission for review, but we will not desk-reject a paper solely because a preprint exists.

Can the organizers submit? How are conflicts of interest handled?

They cannot. Organizers, and anyone with a personal conflict of interest with an organizer, may not submit. Organizers do not give talks and do not assess conflicted submissions, and no reviewer evaluates a submission from their own institution or a close collaborator.
